3 — DEFINITION

<h2><strong>Definition</strong></h2>  
<p>👉 <strong>Homeostasis is the process by which the body regulates its internal conditions to maintain a stable, optimum environment for cells and enzymes despite changes inside or outside the body.</strong></p>  

4 — WHY IT MATTERS  
Paste into: Text (concept)

<h2><strong>Why it matters</strong></h2>  
<p>👉 <strong>This matters because enzymes and cells only work efficiently within narrow conditions, so homeostasis keeps the body healthy and functioning well.</strong></p>  

5 — CORE MODEL  
Paste into: Core rule (key idea)

<h2><strong>Core model</strong></h2>  
<p>👉 <strong>The basic model of homeostasis involves three key parts working in a sequence:</strong></p>  
<ol>  
<li><strong>Receptors</strong> detect changes (stimuli) in internal conditions.</li>  
<li><strong>Coordination centres</strong> (like the brain, spinal cord, or pancreas) process and interpret this information.</li>  
<li><strong>Effectors</strong> (muscles or glands) respond to restore the condition to its optimum level.</li>  
</ol>  
<p>Step-by-step chain: Stimulus → Receptor → Coordination centre → Effector → Response → Return to optimum</p>  

6 — KEY EXAMPLES  
Paste into: Text (concept)

<h2><strong>Key examples</strong></h2>  
<ul>  
<li><strong>Body temperature regulation:</strong> Thermoreceptors detect temperature changes, the hypothalamus coordinates, and effectors like sweat glands cool the body.</li>  
<li><strong>Blood glucose control:</strong> Receptors in the pancreas detect glucose levels, release hormones like insulin or glucagon to effectors (liver, muscles) to adjust glucose.</li>  
<li><strong>Water balance:</strong> Osmoreceptors detect blood water levels; the brain and kidneys adjust water reabsorption through effectors.</li>  
</ul>

9 — CORE TEACHING  
Paste into: Text (concept)

<h2><strong>Teaching: Homeostasis</strong></h2>  
<p>👉 The key idea is that homeostasis controls internal conditions like temperature and glucose by a feedback system.</p>  

<h3><strong>What happens:</strong></h3>  
<ul>  
<li>The body detects any change (stimulus) away from the ideal condition (like temperature rising).</li>  
<li>Receptors sense this change and send information to coordination centres.</li>  
<li>The coordination centre processes the information and sends instructions to effectors.</li>  
<li>Effectors carry out a response that reverses the change and brings conditions back to the optimum.</li>  
</ul>  

<h3><strong>Structure → function / Process → effect:</strong></h3>  
<ul>  
<li>The <strong>thermoreceptors</strong> in skin and brain detect temperature changes → this triggers the <strong>hypothalamus</strong> (the coordination centre) to activate <strong>sweat glands</strong> → sweat evaporates from the skin surface → evaporation removes heat energy → body temperature returns to optimum.</li>  
</ul>  

<h3><strong>Think like an examiner:</strong></h3>  
<p>👉 Don’t just say "body gets hot and sweats". Instead, say "Thermoreceptors detect a rise in core temperature, the hypothalamus coordinates the response, sweat glands produce sweat which evaporates and cools the body back to the optimum temperature."</p>

12 — COMMON MISTAKE  
Paste into: Common mistake

<h2><strong>Common mistake</strong></h2>  
<p>👉 A common mistake is to say "The brain makes you sweat" without naming the hypothalamus as the coordination centre.</p>  

<p><strong>Why this costs marks:</strong> AQA examiners expect precise terms. Saying just “brain” is too vague and missing the key biological term.</p>  

<p>👉 A better way is: “Thermoreceptors detect temperature change, the hypothalamus processes this information, and sweat glands produce sweat to cool the body.”</p>  

<p>👉 Avoid: “Sweating cools you down” without explaining that evaporation removes heat energy.</p>

17 — WORKED EXAMPLE  
Paste into: Worked example (checkpoint)

Question:  
Explain how the body responds when blood glucose levels are too high. (4 marks)  

Answer:  
<details>  
<summary>Reveal Answer</summary>  

1. Blood glucose levels rise above the optimum (stimulus).  
2. Receptors in the pancreas detect this change.  
3. The pancreas acts as the coordination centre and releases insulin (effector stimulus).  
4. Insulin causes liver and muscle cells to absorb glucose and store it as glycogen (response).  
5. Blood glucose levels fall back to the optimum.  

This sequence shows homeostasis restoring balance after a change.  
</details>

24 — KEY WORDS  
Paste into: Key words

<h2><strong>Key words</strong></h2>  
<strong>Homeostasis</strong> – The regulation of internal conditions to maintain a stable environment.  
<strong>Stimulus</strong> – A change in the internal or external environment.  
<strong>Receptor</strong> – A cell or group of cells that detect a stimulus.  
<strong>Coordination centre</strong> – Processes information from receptors and sends instructions to effectors.  
<strong>Effector</strong> – A muscle or gland that produces a response to restore the optimum condition.  
<strong>Response</strong> – The action taken by effectors to correct a change.  
<strong>Optimum</strong> – The best or ideal conditions for enzyme and cell function.  
<strong>Negative feedback</strong> – A process that reverses a change and returns conditions to optimum.  
<strong>Hormone</strong> – A chemical messenger released by glands into the blood to affect cells elsewhere.  
<strong>Enzyme</strong> – A protein that speeds up biological reactions, sensitive to environmental conditions.
