3 — DEFINITION  
Paste into: Text (concept)

<h2><strong>Definition</strong></h2>  
<p>👉 <strong>Electric current is the flow of electric charge (usually electrons) through a conductor, measured in amperes (A).</strong></p>

<p>👉 <strong>Potential difference (voltage) across a component is the energy transferred per unit charge, measured in volts (V).</strong></p>

<p>👉 <strong>Resistance is a measure of how much a component opposes the flow of current, measured in ohms (Ω).</strong></p>  

4 — WHY IT MATTERS  
Paste into: Text (concept)

<h2><strong>🌍 Why this matters</strong></h2>  
<p>👉 <strong>Electricity powers almost all our devices—from lights to phones—and understanding current, voltage, and resistance helps us explain how these devices work and how to use electricity safely and efficiently.</strong></p>  

5 — CORE MODEL  
Paste into: Core rule (key idea)

<p>👉 <strong>Electric circuits consist of a power source pushing charge (current) through components that use energy; the potential difference (voltage) drives the current, and resistance reduces current flow following Ohm’s Law: V = IR.</strong></p>  

6 — KEY EXAMPLES  
Paste into: Text (concept)

<ul>  
<li>Series circuit: current constant, voltage divides among components.</li>  
<li>Parallel circuit: voltage constant across branches, current divides.</li>  
<li>Resistor obeying Ohm’s Law: voltage proportional to current.</li>  
<li>Filament bulb: resistance increases with temperature, so doesn’t obey Ohm’s Law perfectly.</li>  
</ul>

9 — CORE TEACHING  
Paste into: Text (concept)

<h2><strong>Teaching: Electricity</strong></h2>  
<p>👉 <strong>Let’s break down how electricity flows through a circuit and what the key quantities mean.</strong></p>

<h3><strong>Key idea:</strong></h3>  
<p>Electric current is the rate of flow of charge, moving because the battery provides energy to push electrons around the circuit.</p>

<h3><strong>What happens:</strong></h3>  
<ul>  
<li>Charge moves through wires and components, carrying energy from the power supply.</li>  
<li>Potential difference (voltage) is the energy given to each coulomb of charge to move through the circuit.</li>  
<li>Resistance within components opposes the flow, reducing current depending on the material and temperature.</li>  
</ul>

<h3><strong>Structure → function / Process → effect:</strong></h3>  
<ul>  
<li>Battery → provides energy → creates potential difference.</li>  
<li>Potential difference → pushes charge → current flows.</li>  
<li>Resistance → opposes current → reduces current size.</li>  
<li>Current × Resistance = Voltage; changes in resistance change current for given voltage.</li>  
</ul>

<h3><strong>Think like an examiner:</strong></h3>  
<p>👉 Use the relationship V = IR to explain how changes in voltage or resistance affect current.</p>  
<p>👉 When describing circuits, link current and voltage changes in series vs parallel clearly.</p>  
<p>👉 Always mention cause → effect and use correct units (A, V, Ω).</p>

12 — COMMON MISTAKE  
Paste into: Common mistake

<p>👉 A common mistake is saying “voltage is used up” like energy disappears inside components.</p>  
<p>👉 The better idea is that voltage is the energy given per coulomb of charge and energy changes form (e.g., electrical to light or heat), but total energy is conserved.</p>  
<p>👉 This correction helps students explain energy transfer in circuits, which gains more marks.</p>

16 — EXAM TIP  
Paste into: Exam tip (concept)

<h2><strong>🌍 Why this matters</strong></h2>  
<p>👉 Understanding how current, voltage, and resistance work in circuits helps you predict how devices behave and solve exam problems with confidence.</p>

<h2><strong>🎯 Premium Exam Tip</strong></h2>  
<p>👉 In exam questions, don’t just state facts like “current decreases.” Always explain <em>why</em>, using cause → effect language with correct terms and units.</p>  
<p>👉 For example: “The resistance increases, so the current decreases because current is inversely proportional to resistance at a constant voltage.”</p>  

<h2><strong>🎯 Answer Quality Modelling</strong></h2>  
<p>👉 This is how to move from a basic answer to a full-mark explanation.</p>  

<h3><strong>Weak answer:</strong></h3>  
<p>The current gets lower in the resistor.</p>  

<h3><strong>Better answer:</strong></h3>  
<p>The current gets lower because the resistor stops some current.</p>  

<h3><strong>Full-mark answer:</strong></h3>  
<p>The current through the resistor is lower because the resistance opposes the flow of charge; according to Ohm’s law, current equals voltage divided by resistance, so increasing resistance reduces current for the same voltage.</p>  

<h3><strong>Why the full-mark answer is stronger:</strong></h3>  
<ul>  
<li>It clearly uses cause → effect linking resistance to current.</li>  
<li>Incorporates Ohm’s Law equation to add scientific precision.</li>  
<li>Avoids vague wording like “stops some current.”</li>  
</ul>  

17 — FINAL MEMORY RULE  
Paste into: Final memory rule (key idea)

<h2><strong>💡 Key Insight</strong></h2>  
<p>👉 In any electric circuit, <strong>Voltage = Current × Resistance (V = IR)</strong>, so increasing resistance lowers current, and increasing voltage increases current, with energy flowing through charge carriers around the circuit.</p>

19 — SYNTHESIS  
Paste into: Synthesis (key idea)

<p>👉 The relationship between current, voltage, and resistance is central to understanding how circuits behave whether components are arranged in series or parallel.</p>  
<p>👉 In series, current remains constant and voltage divides; in parallel, voltage remains constant and current divides through each branch.</p>  
<p>👉 Resistance totals differently in each arrangement, affecting overall current and circuit function.</p>  

20 — SELF-CHECK QUESTION  
Paste into: Self-check question

Question:  
Explain why the current is the same at every point in a series circuit.

Answer:  
<details>  
<summary>Reveal Answer</summary>  
Because charge has only one path to travel, the same amount of charge flows past every point per second, so the current is constant throughout a series circuit.  
</details>  

21 — FINAL MEMORY RULE  
Paste into: Final memory rule (key idea)

<h2><strong>💡 Key Insight</strong></h2>  
<p>👉 Remember that in series circuits, current is constant everywhere and voltage is shared, while in parallel circuits, voltage is the same across branches and current splits according to resistance.</p>  

22 — EXAM PRACTICE  
Paste into: Text (concept)

<p>👉 Practice exam questions to apply your knowledge and build exam technique.</p>

<p>Q1 (1 mark): What unit is electric current measured in?</p>

<p>Q2 (2 marks): A circuit has a 12 V battery and a resistor of 4 Ω. Calculate the current using Ohm’s Law.</p>

<p>Q3 (3 marks): Explain how adding more resistors in series affects the total resistance and current in the circuit.</p>  

<details>  
<summary>Reveal Model Answer Q3</summary>  
Adding resistors in series increases the total resistance because resistances add up. This higher resistance reduces the total current in the circuit according to Ohm’s Law (I = V / R).  
</details>  

<p>Q4 (4 marks): A lamp and a resistor are connected in parallel across a 6 V battery. Compare the current through the lamp and the resistor, and explain why they differ.</p>  

<details>  
<summary>Reveal Model Answer Q4</summary>  
The voltage across the lamp and resistor is the same because they are in parallel. However, their currents differ because each component has a different resistance. According to Ohm’s Law (I = V / R), the component with lower resistance will have a higher current. Therefore, the current is larger in the component with less resistance.  
</details>

<ul>

24 — KEY WORDS  
Paste into: Key words

<strong>Current</strong> – The rate of flow of electric charge, measured in amperes (A).  
<strong>Potential difference</strong> – Energy transferred per unit charge through a component, measured in volts (V).  
<strong>Resistance</strong> – A component’s opposition to current flow, measured in ohms (Ω).  
<strong>Ohm’s Law</strong> – The rule V = IR relating voltage, current, and resistance.  
<strong>Series circuit</strong> – A circuit where components are connected end-to-end, current is the same.  
<strong>Parallel circuit</strong> – A circuit where components are connected in separate branches, voltage is the same.  
<strong>Ammeter</strong> – A device measuring current, connected in series.  
<strong>Voltmeter</strong> – A device measuring voltage, connected in parallel across a component.  
<strong>Voltage (V)</strong> – A synonym for potential difference, the driving force pushing current.  
<strong>Resistor</strong> – A component that provides resistance in a circuit, controlling current flow.
