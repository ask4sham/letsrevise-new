5 — CORE MODEL  
Paste into: Core rule (key idea)

<p>👉 <strong>Electric circuits are organised by connecting components either in series (one after another) or in parallel (side by side), which determines how current and voltage behave.</strong></p>  

<p>Core relationships to remember:</p>  
<ol>  
<li>In series circuits: current is the same through all components, total voltage divides between components.</li>  
<li>In parallel circuits: voltage is the same across each branch, but current divides between branches.</li>  
<li>Total resistance in series adds up; in parallel, total resistance is less than the smallest individual resistor.</li>  
<li>Ohm’s Law applies locally to components: V = IR.</li>  
</ol>

9 — CORE TEACHING  
Paste into: Text (concept)

<h2><strong>Teaching: Electricity Circuit Organisation</strong></h2>  
<p>👉 <strong>The key idea is how arrangement affects current and voltage.</strong></p>  

<h3><strong>Current and voltage in series</strong></h3>  
<ul>  
<li>In series circuits, there is only one path, so the <strong>current is the same</strong> through every component.</li>  
<li>The <strong>total voltage of the supply splits</strong> between the components depending on their resistance.</li>  
<li>This means bulbs in series share the battery voltage equally if identical.</li>  
</ul>  

<h3><strong>Current and voltage in parallel</strong></h3>  
<ul>  
<li>In parallel circuits, components are connected along separate branches.</li>  
<li>The <strong>voltage across each branch is equal</strong> to the full supply voltage.</li>  
<li>The current from the battery <strong>splits depending on resistance</strong>; more current flows through lower resistance branches.</li>  
</ul>  

<h3><strong>Resistance in series and parallel</strong></h3>  
<ul>  
<li>For components in series: <strong>total resistance = sum of individual resistances</strong> (Rₜ = R₁ + R₂ + ...).</li>  
<li>For components in parallel: <strong>total resistance is less than the smallest resistor</strong>, calculated by 1/Rₜ = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + ...</li>  
</ul>  

<h3><strong>Think like an examiner</strong></h3>  
<p>👉 Always explain current and voltage separately and relate them to the circuit layout. Use “because” to link cause and effect: for example, “The current is the same because there is only one path.”</p>

12 — COMMON MISTAKE  
Paste into: Common mistake

<p>👉 <strong>A common mistake is saying “current is used up” as it flows through components in series.</strong></p>  
<p>The correct idea is that <strong>current is the same all through a series circuit</strong> because charge flow is continuous and conserved.</p>  
<p>👉 <strong>Why this matters:</strong> Saying current is “used up” would lose marks because it shows misunderstanding of current flow and charge conservation.</p>

14 — STEP-BY-STEP PROCESS  
Paste into: Step-by-step diagram (process)

Process:  
Calculating total resistance in series and parallel circuits  

Step 1:  
In series, add up all resistances: Rₜ = R₁ + R₂ + R₃ + ...

↓

Step 2:  
In parallel, calculate reciprocal of resistances: 1/Rₜ = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + 1/R₃ + ...

↓

Step 3:  
Find total current using I = V / Rₜ with the supply voltage V and total resistance Rₜ.

↓

Step 4:  
Use current and/or resistance to calculate unknown voltages or currents in each component using V = IR or rules of current/voltage in series/parallel.

<p>👉 In exam questions, don’t just state trends like “current increases” — always explain <em>why</em> using cause → effect (e.g., “Current increases because resistance decreases, allowing more charge to flow through each second”).</p>  
<p>👉 Always mention the relationship between components' arrangement, voltage, and current using correct terms like series or parallel.</p>  

<h2><strong>🎯 Answer Quality Modelling</strong></h2>  
<p>👉 This is how to move from a basic answer to a full-mark explanation.</p>  

<h3><strong>Weak answer:</strong></h3>  
<p>Current is the same in series.</p>  

<h3><strong>Better answer:</strong></h3>  
<p>Current is the same in series because there is only one path for electrons to flow.</p>  

<h3><strong>Full-mark answer:</strong></h3>  
<p>Current is the same through all components in a series circuit because charge cannot be used up; the same number of electrons pass every point per second as there is only one path available.</p>  

<h3><strong>Why the full-mark answer is stronger:</strong></h3>  
<ul>  
<li>It explains the physical cause (only one path, charge conservation).</li>  
<li>It links terminology (electrons, series circuit) with the reasoning.</li>  
<li>It avoids vague terms and incorrect ideas like current being “used up.”</li>  
</ul>  

17 — WORKED EXAMPLE  
Paste into: Worked example (checkpoint)

Question:  
A 12 V battery is connected to two resistors, 4 Ω and 8 Ω, in series. Calculate:  
a) The total resistance.  
b) The current flowing through the circuit.  
c) The voltage across the 8 Ω resistor.  

Answer:  
<details>  
<summary>Reveal Answer</summary>  
a) Total resistance in series: Rₜ = 4 Ω + 8 Ω = 12 Ω  

b) Using Ohm’s Law: I = V / Rₜ = 12 V / 12 Ω = 1 A  

c) Voltage across 8 Ω resistor: V = IR = 1 A × 8 Ω = 8 V  
</details>

22 — EXAM PRACTICE  
Paste into: Text (concept)

<p><strong>Q1 (1 mark):</strong> Define electric current.</p>  
<p><strong>Q2 (2 marks):</strong> Explain what happens to current in a circuit when more resistors are added in series.</p>  
<p><strong>Q3 (3 marks):</strong> A parallel circuit has a 6 V supply and two branches: one resistor of 3 Ω and another resistor of 6 Ω. Calculate the current in each branch.</p>  
<p><strong>Q4 (4 marks):</strong> Compare the current and voltage in series and parallel circuits and explain how adding more bulbs affects the brightness in each arrangement.</p>  

<details>  
<summary>Reveal Model Answers</summary>  

Q1:  
Electric current is the flow of electric charge, usually electrons, around a circuit.  

Q2:  
Adding more resistors in series increases total resistance because resistances add up. This causes current to decrease because current = voltage / resistance.  

Q3:  
Total voltage = 6 V (same across both branches).  
Current in 3 Ω resistor branch: I = V / R = 6 V / 3 Ω = 2 A.  
Current in 6 Ω resistor branch: I = 6 V / 6 Ω = 1 A.  

Q4:  
In series, current is the same through all components but voltage divides, so adding bulbs lowers voltage per bulb, dimming them. In parallel, voltage is equal across bulbs, so brightness stays same but total current increases. Adding bulbs adds branches, allowing more current overall.  
</details>

24 — KEY WORDS  
Paste into: Key words

<p><strong>Current</strong> – The flow of electric charge measured in amperes (A).</p>  
<p><strong>Voltage</strong> – The energy transferred per charge, measured in volts (V).</p>  
<p><strong>Resistance</strong> – How much a component opposes the flow of current, measured in ohms (Ω).</p>  
<p><strong>Series circuit</strong> – Components connected in a single path.</p>  
<p><strong>Parallel circuit</strong> – Components connected on separate branches.</p>  
<p><strong>Ohm’s Law</strong> – The relationship V = IR between voltage, current, and resistance.</p>  
<p><strong>Total resistance</strong> – The combined resistance of all components in a circuit.</p>  
<p><strong>Battery</strong> – A source of electrical energy creating potential difference.</p>  
<p><strong>Current splits</strong> – In parallel circuits, current divides between different paths.</p>  
<p><strong>Voltage splits</strong> – In series circuits, voltage divides between components.</p>
