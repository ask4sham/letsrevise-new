9 — CORE TEACHING  
Paste into: Text (concept)

<h2><strong>Teaching: Causes of WW1</strong></h2>  
<p>👉 Right, let’s break this down step-by-step. The causes of WW1 aren’t just one thing, but several interlinked factors.</p>  

<h3><strong>Key idea:</strong></h3>  
<p>The outbreak of WW1 resulted from long-term tensions, rising rivalries, and a final trigger event creating a chain reaction across Europe.</p>  

<h3><strong>What happens:</strong></h3>  
<ul>  
<li><strong>Militarism:</strong> Countries built up their armies and navies aggressively—this arms race made everyone ready and willing to fight.</li>  
<li><strong>Alliances:</strong> Two big blocs formed, so when Austria-Hungary declared war on Serbia, their allies joined in quickly, spreading the conflict.</li>  
<li><strong>Imperialism:</strong> Competition for colonies heightened suspicion and conflict between major powers.</li>  
<li><strong>Nationalism:</strong> Intense pride and desire for independence fuelled tensions, especially in the Balkans.</li>  
<li>The <strong>assassination</strong> of Archduke Franz Ferdinand was the immediate spark that made all these tensions explode into war.</li>  
</ul>  

<h3><strong>Structure → effect chain:</strong></h3>  
<ul>  
<li>Militarism → arms build-up → fear and readiness to fight → increased tension</li>  
<li>Alliances → commitments between countries → local conflict turns into wider war</li>  
<li>Imperialism → competition for colonies → rivalry and distrust</li>  
<li>Nationalism → ethnic tensions → desire for independence or dominance → conflicts</li>  
<li>Assassination → crisis → declaration of war → chain reaction of mobilisations</li>  
</ul>  

<h3><strong>Think like an examiner:</strong></h3>  
<p>👉 In your answer, always explain why one cause led to another and how it made war more likely. Use terms like ‘because’, ‘which caused’, or ‘as a result’ to show clear links.</p>

12 — COMMON MISTAKE  
Paste into: Common mistake

<p>👉 A common mistake is saying “The war started because Germany just wanted to fight.”</p>  
<p>👉 The better way to say it is that Germany was part of rising tensions like militarism and alliances, and a specific event—the assassination—triggered war.</p>  
<p>👉 This correction earns marks because it explains the wider causes, not just blaming one country without context.</p>

16 — EXAM TIP  
Paste into: Exam tip (concept)

<h2><strong>🌍 Why this matters</strong></h2>  
<p>👉 Understanding WW1 causes helps you explain how a local conflict escalated into a global war through alliances and rivalries.</p>  

<h2><strong>🎯 Premium Exam Tip</strong></h2>  
<p>👉 In the exam, avoid just listing causes. Always explain <em>why</em> each cause made war more likely and use the cause → effect → consequence structure.</p>  

<h2><strong>🎯 Answer Quality Modelling</strong></h2>  
<p>👉 This is how to move from a basic answer to a full-mark explanation.</p>  

<h3><strong>Weak answer:</strong></h3>  
<p>World War One started because countries had alliances.</p>  

<h3><strong>Better answer:</strong></h3>  
<p>World War One started because countries had alliances, so when one country was attacked, their allies joined the fight.</p>  

<h3><strong>Full-mark answer:</strong></h3>  
<p>World War One started because the alliance system forced countries to support each other quickly, which caused a local conflict to escalate into a widespread war as more countries mobilised their armies.</p>  

<h3><strong>Why the full-mark answer is stronger:</strong></h3>  
<ul>  
<li>It explains the cause and effect clearly.</li>  
<li>It uses precise terms like “alliance system” and “mobilised”.</li>  
<li>It shows understanding of how a local conflict became a European war.</li>  
</ul>

24 — KEY WORDS  
Paste into: Key words

<strong>Militarism</strong> – The policy of building up strong armed forces to prepare for war.  
<strong>Alliances</strong> – Agreements between countries to support each other in case of attack.  
<strong>Imperialism</strong> – When countries compete to take control over other lands and colonies.  
<strong>Nationalism</strong> – Extreme pride in one’s country or ethnic group, sometimes leading to conflict.  
<strong>Assassination</strong> – The murder of a prominent person for political reasons.  
<strong>Arms race</strong> – Competition between countries to have the most powerful weapons.  
<strong>Balance of Power</strong> – A situation where countries have equal strength to prevent any one from dominating.  
<strong>Mobilisation</strong> – The process of preparing and organising troops for war.  
<strong>Trigger</strong> – An event that sets off a bigger reaction.  
<strong>Chain reaction</strong> – A series of events, each caused by the previous one, leading to a final outcome.
