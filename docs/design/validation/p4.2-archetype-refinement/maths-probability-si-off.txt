3 — DEFINITION  
Paste into: Text (concept)  

<h2><strong>Definition</strong></h2>  
<p>👉 <strong>Probability</strong> is a number between 0 and 1 that measures how likely an event is to happen.</p>  
<p>A probability of 0 means an event cannot happen, and a probability of 1 means it is certain to happen.</p>  

4 — WHY IT MATTERS  
Paste into: Text (concept)  

<h2><strong>🌍 Why this matters</strong></h2>  
<p>👉 Probability helps us predict and understand outcomes in daily life, from weather forecasts to games of chance and real-world decisions.</p>  

5 — CORE MODEL  
Paste into: Core rule (key idea)  

<p>👉 <strong>Probability of an event = (Number of favourable outcomes) ÷ (Total number of possible outcomes)</strong></p>  
<p>This formula is the core for calculating probability when all outcomes are equally likely.</p>  

6 — KEY EXAMPLES  
Paste into: Text (concept)  

<ul>  
<li>Flipping a fair coin: probability of heads is 1 favourable outcome ÷ 2 possible = ½.</li>  
<li>Rolling a six-sided die: probability of rolling a 4 is 1 ÷ 6.</li>  
<li>Picking a red card from a standard 52-card deck: 26 red cards ÷ 52 cards = ½ probability.</li>  
</ul>

9 — CORE TEACHING  
Paste into: Text (concept)  

<h2><strong>Teaching: Probability</strong></h2>  
<p>👉 The key idea is: Probability measures how likely something is to happen, from impossible (0) to certain (1).</p>  
<h3><strong>What happens:</strong></h3>  
<ul>  
<li>The sample space lists every possible outcome.</li>  
<li>The probability of an event = number of favourable outcomes ÷ total outcomes.</li>  
<li>Probabilities are always between 0 and 1 (including).</li>  
</ul>  
<h3><strong>Structure → function / Process → effect:</strong></h3>  
<ul>  
<li>Identify all possible outcomes (sample space).</li>  
<li>Count favourable outcomes for the event.</li>  
<li>Divide favourable outcomes by total to find probability.</li>  
<li>Use addition or multiplication rules for combined events as needed.</li>  
</ul>  
<h3><strong>Think like an examiner:</strong></h3>  
<p>👉 Always state probability as a fraction, decimal, or percentage between 0 and 1. For combined events, specify if events are independent or mutually exclusive to apply correct rules.</p>

12 — COMMON MISTAKE  
Paste into: Common mistake  

<p>👉 A common mistake is adding probabilities of all outcomes without checking if they are mutually exclusive.</p>  
<p>Students often confuse independent and mutually exclusive events, wrongly mixing addition and multiplication rules.</p>  
<p>Do NOT say: "The probability of event A and B is the sum of their probabilities" if events can happen together.</p>  
<p>A better way to say it is: Use addition for mutually exclusive events, multiplication for independent events.</p>  
<p>This earns marks by applying correct rules for combined probabilities.</p>

16 — EXAM TIP  
Paste into: Exam tip (concept)  

<h2><strong>🌍 Why this matters</strong></h2>  
<p>👉 Understanding probability is crucial because exams often require you to calculate the chance of combined events and justify your answers clearly.</p>  

<h2><strong>🎯 Premium Exam Tip</strong></h2>  
<p>Always state whether events are independent or mutually exclusive before applying addition or multiplication rules. This precision often earns method marks.</p>  

<h2><strong>🎯 Answer Quality Modelling</strong></h2>  
<p>👉 This is how to move from a basic answer to a full-mark explanation.</p>  

<h3><strong>Weak answer:</strong></h3>  
<p>The probability of rolling a 4 is one out of six.</p>  

<h3><strong>Better answer:</strong></h3>  
<p>The probability of rolling a 4 is 1/6 because there is one 4 and six possible numbers on the die.</p>  

<h3><strong>Full-mark answer:</strong></h3>  
<p>The probability of rolling a 4 on a fair six-sided die is 1/6, calculated by dividing the one favourable outcome (rolling a 4) by the total outcomes (numbers 1 to 6), assuming all outcomes are equally likely.</p>  

<h3><strong>Why the full-mark answer is stronger:</strong></h3>  
<ul>  
<li>It clearly identifies the favourable and total outcomes.</li>  
<li>It links cause (one 4) to process (division) and effect (probability value).</li>  
<li>It uses precise vocabulary and assumptions about fairness and equal likelihood.</li>  
</ul>  

17 — WORKED EXAMPLE  
Paste into: Worked example (checkpoint)  

Question:  
A bag contains 3 green balls, 2 yellow balls, and 5 red balls. What is the probability of picking a yellow or red ball?  

Answer:  
<details>  
<summary>Reveal Answer</summary>  

Total balls = 3 + 2 + 5 = 10  
Favourable outcomes = yellow + red = 2 + 5 = 7  
Since yellow and red balls are mutually exclusive, add the probabilities:  
Probability = 7 ÷ 10 = 0.7 or 7/10  
</details>

<h2><strong>Synthesis</strong></h2>  
<p>👉 Probability is calculated by counting how many ways an event can happen (favourable outcomes) compared to all possible ways, with results between 0 and 1.</p>  
<p>👉 Combined events require knowing whether to add or multiply probabilities based on mutual exclusivity or independence.</p>

20 — SELF-CHECK QUESTION  
Paste into: Self-check question  

Question:  
A fair coin is flipped twice. What is the probability of getting two heads?  

Answer:  
<details>  
<summary>Reveal Answer</summary>  

Probability of head on first flip = 1/2  
Probability of head on second flip = 1/2  
For independent events, multiply probabilities:  
(1/2) × (1/2) = 1/4  
</details>  

21 — FINAL MEMORY RULE  
Paste into: Final memory rule (key idea)  

<h2><strong>💡 Key Insight</strong></h2>  
<p>👉 Probability = (Favourable outcomes) ÷ (Total outcomes) always between 0 and 1. For combined events, use:</p>  
<ul>  
<li>Add for mutually exclusive events (either-or situations).</li>  
<li>Multiply for independent events (both happen together).</li>  
</ul>  
<p>Always define event type before choosing the rule.</p>  

22 — EXAM PRACTICE  
Paste into: Text (concept)  

<p><strong>Q1 (1 mark):</strong> Find the probability of rolling a 2 on a fair six-sided die.</p>  
<p><strong>Q2 (2 marks):</strong> A box contains 4 red, 3 blue, and 5 green marbles. Calculate the probability of picking a red marble.</p>  
<p><strong>Q3 (3 marks):</strong> Two coins are flipped. What is the probability of getting exactly one head? Show your working.</p>  
<p><strong>Q4 (4 marks):</strong> A bag contains 6 black and 4 white balls. Two balls are picked one after the other without replacement. Calculate the probability both balls are black.</p>  

<details>  
<summary>Reveal Model Answers</summary>  

Q1:  
Probability = 1/6  

Q2:  
Total marbles = 4 + 3 + 5 = 12  
Probability red = 4/12 = 1/3  

Q3:  
Sample space: HH, HT, TH, TT  
Favourable outcomes: HT, TH = 2  
Probability = 2/4 = 1/2  

Q4:  
First black ball: 6/10  
Second black ball (no replacement): 5/9  
Probability = (6/10) × (5/9) = 30/90 = 1/3  
</details>

24 — KEY WORDS  
Paste into: Key words  

<strong>Event</strong> – The outcome or set of outcomes we are interested in.  
<strong>Outcome</strong> – A possible result of an experiment.  
<strong>Sample space</strong> – The set of all possible outcomes.  
<strong>Favourable outcomes</strong> – Outcomes that count toward the event.  
<strong>Equally likely</strong> – When all outcomes have the same chance of happening.  
<strong>Independent events</strong> – Events where one does not affect the probability of the other.  
<strong>Mutually exclusive</strong> – Events that cannot happen at the same time.  
<strong>Probability</strong> – A number between 0 and 1 showing how likely an event is.  
<strong>Complement</strong> – The probability that an event does not happen.  
<strong>Tree diagram</strong> – A visual tool used to list all possible outcomes of combined events and their probabilities.
