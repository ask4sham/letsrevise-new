9 — CORE TEACHING  
Paste into: Text (concept)

<h2><strong>Teaching: Forces</strong></h2>  
<p>👉 The key idea is that forces always come with direction as well as size; they are vector quantities.</p>  
<h3><strong>What happens:</strong></h3>  
<ul>  
<li>If the forces on an object are all balanced (add up to zero), the object stays still or keeps moving at the same speed.</li>  
<li>If the forces are unbalanced, the object accelerates in the direction of the bigger force.</li>  
</ul>  
<h3><strong>Structure → function / Process → effect:</strong></h3>  
<ul>  
<li>Draw arrows to represent forces: length shows size, arrowhead shows direction.</li>  
<li>Add forces in the same direction, subtract opposing forces to find resultant force.</li>  
<li>Use resultant force to predict motion changes.</li>  
</ul>  
<h3><strong>Think like an examiner:</strong></h3>  
<p>👉 Always explain the sizes and directions of forces, then say how the resultant force causes acceleration or no acceleration. Use clear terms like “resultant force” and relate this directly to motion.</p>

12 — COMMON MISTAKE  
Paste into: Common mistake

<p>👉 A common mistake is to think forces are only sizes and ignore direction.</p>  
<p>This leads to errors when adding forces, especially if they act in opposite directions.</p>  
<p>Remember forces are vectors: you must subtract opposite forces, not just add all sizes.</p>  
<p>Think of it like tug-of-war — the winners pull harder, so net force is the difference.</p>

16 — EXAM TIP  
Paste into: Exam tip (concept)

<h2><strong>🌍 Why this matters</strong></h2>  
<p>👉 Many GCSE questions require you to explain how forces affect motion or to calculate resultant forces.</p>  
<p>Understanding balanced and unbalanced forces is key to answering these correctly, showing cause and effect clearly.</p>

<h2><strong>🎯 Premium Exam Tip</strong></h2>  
<p>👉 Always give the direction and size of forces in your answer, then explain how the resultant force changes the motion — this gets full marks.</p>  
<p>👉 In calculations, check units and use subtraction for opposing forces — do not just add all numbers.</p>

<h2><strong>🎯 Answer Quality Modelling</strong></h2>  
<p>👉 This is how to move from a basic answer to a full-mark explanation.</p>

<h3><strong>Weak answer:</strong></h3>  
<p>Forces can make an object move.</p>

<h3><strong>Better answer:</strong></h3>  
<p>Forces can change how fast an object moves because they push or pull it.</p>

<h3><strong>Full-mark answer:</strong></h3>  
<p>A resultant force acts on the object when the forces are unbalanced; this causes acceleration in the direction of the resultant force, changing the object’s speed or direction.</p>

<h3><strong>Why the full-mark answer is stronger:</strong></h3>  
<ul>  
<li>It explains the concept of resultant force clearly.</li>  
<li>It links force unbalance to acceleration (cause → effect).</li>  
<li>Uses precise GCSE terminology to fulfil mark scheme requirements.</li>  
</ul>  

17 — WORKED EXAMPLE  
Paste into: Worked example (checkpoint)

Question:  
A box is pulled to the right with a force of 30 N. Friction acts to the left with a force of 12 N. Calculate the resultant force and state the direction of movement.

Answer:  
<details>  
<summary>Reveal Answer</summary>  

Resultant force = 30 N – 12 N = 18 N to the right.  
Since the resultant force is positive to the right, the box will accelerate to the right.  
</details>

<h2><strong>Synthesis: Forces and Motion</strong></h2>  
<p>👉 Forces are vector quantities that affect motion depending on their size and direction.</p>  
<p>Balancing forces means no change in speed or direction, while unbalanced forces cause acceleration or deceleration.</p>  
<p>Resultant force lets us summarise all forces into one force that tells us what motion will happen.</p>

22 — EXAM PRACTICE  
Paste into: Text (concept)

<p>Q1 (1 mark): Define a force.</p>  
<p>Q2 (2 marks): Describe what happens when forces on an object are balanced.</p>  
<p>Q3 (3 marks): A cyclist pedals forward with a force of 100 N while air resistance and friction together exert 30 N against the cyclist. Calculate the resultant force and explain what happens.</p>  
<p>Q4 (4 marks): Explain why a stationary box on a slope doesn’t slide down if the forces acting on it are balanced. Include forces involved and what would happen if one force changed.</p>

<details>  
<summary>Reveal Model Answers</summary>  

Q1 Answer:  
A force is a push or pull that can change the motion or shape of an object.

Q2 Answer:  
Balanced forces mean the total force is zero, so the object does not change its motion; it stays still or moves at constant speed.

Q3 Answer:  
Resultant force = 100 N – 30 N = 70 N forward.  
This unbalanced force causes the cyclist to accelerate forward.

Q4 Answer:  
The box has weight pulling it down the slope, and friction and the normal force pushing up against it. When these forces balance, the resultant force is zero, so the box does not move. If friction decreased, the unbalanced force downhill would cause the box to slide down.
</details>

24 — KEY WORDS  
Paste into: Key words

<strong>Force</strong> – A push or pull acting on an object with size and direction.  
<strong>Resultant force</strong> – Single force representing the combined effect of all forces.  
<strong>Balanced forces</strong> – Forces that cancel each other producing zero resultant force.  
<strong>Unbalanced forces</strong> – Forces that do not cancel, causing acceleration.  
<strong>Tension</strong> – Pulling force in a string, rope, or cable.  
<strong>Friction</strong> – Force opposing motion between surfaces in contact.  
<strong>Weight</strong> – Force due to gravity acting on an object’s mass.  
<strong>Vector</strong> – Quantity that has both magnitude and direction.  
<strong>Contact force</strong> – Force from objects physically touching.  
<strong>Non-contact force</strong> – Force acting at a distance, e.g. gravity.
