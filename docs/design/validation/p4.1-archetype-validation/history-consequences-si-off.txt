9 — CORE TEACHING  
Paste into: Text (concept)  

<h2><strong>Teaching: Treaty of Versailles</strong></h2>  
<p>👉 The key idea is understanding how the Treaty was designed to punish Germany and keep peace, but also sowed seeds of future conflict.</p>  

<h3><strong>What happens:</strong></h3>  
<ul>  
<li>The Treaty was signed in June 1919 at the Palace of Versailles by the Allied powers and Germany.</li>  
<li>The “Big Three” leaders each wanted different outcomes:  
<ul>  
<li>David Lloyd George (Britain) wanted to weaken Germany but keep it trading.</li>  
<li>Georges Clemenceau (France) wanted to punish Germany and secure France’s borders.</li>  
<li>Woodrow Wilson (USA) pushed for a “fair peace” and the League of Nations.</li>  
</ul>  
</li>  
<li>Germany had no say in the negotiations – it was a diktat (imposed peace).</li>  
</ul>  

<h3><strong>Structure → function / Process → effect:</strong></h3>  
<ul>  
<li>War Guilt Clause (Article 231): Germany forced to accept responsibility → justified reparations payments → economic hardship in Germany.</li>  
<li>Reparations: Huge debt → weakened Germany’s economy → contributed to hyperinflation and poverty.</li>  
<li>Territorial changes: Lost important regions like Alsace-Lorraine → loss of resources and pride → German resentment.</li>  
<li>Military restrictions: Army limited to 100,000 men → loss of defence capability → Germany felt vulnerable.</li>  
<li>League of Nations created → aimed to prevent future wars → Germany initially excluded, increasing isolation.</li>  
</ul>  

<h3><strong>Think like an examiner:</strong></h3>  
<p>👉 When you explain 'why' the Treaty caused problems, always link a specific Treaty term to its effect on Germany or Europe, and then the consequence, e.g., economic hardship → political instability → rise of extremist parties.</p>

12 — COMMON MISTAKE  
Paste into: Common mistake  

<p>👉 A common mistake is saying the Treaty of Versailles ended WW1. In fact, the armistice ended the fighting in 1918, while the Treaty officially ended the war with terms in 1919.</p>  

<p>Also, do NOT say all Allies agreed on the Treaty terms; there were major disagreements especially between Clemenceau wanting harsh terms and Wilson wanting fairness.</p>  

<p>👉 Correct these and you will earn clearer, more precise marks.</p>

16 — EXAM TIP  
Paste into: Exam tip (concept)  

<h2><strong>🌍 Why this matters</strong></h2>  
<p>👉 The Treaty of Versailles is critical for understanding the causes of future events, including the rise of Hitler and World War Two. Without knowing how harsh terms caused resentment, you can’t fully explain the interwar years.</p>  

<h2><strong>🎯 Premium Exam Tip</strong></h2>  
<p>👉 In the exam, don’t simply list Treaty terms. Explain <em>why</em> each term mattered for Germany and Europe. Use cause → effect → consequence chains. For example, “Because Germany had to pay reparations, this caused economic problems, which made people angry and helped extremist parties rise.”</p>  

<h2><strong>🎯 Answer Quality Modelling</strong></h2>  
<p>👉 This is how to move from a basic answer to a full-mark explanation.</p>  

<h3><strong>Weak answer:</strong></h3>  
<p>The Treaty of Versailles was harsh on Germany.</p>  

<h3><strong>Better answer:</strong></h3>  
<p>The Treaty of Versailles was harsh on Germany because it made them pay money, which hurt their economy.</p>  

<h3><strong>Full-mark answer:</strong></h3>  
<p>The Treaty of Versailles forced Germany to accept guilt for the war (War Guilt Clause), pay huge reparations, and lost territory. This caused economic hardship and loss of pride, leading to political instability and anger that helped extremist parties like the Nazis rise.</p>  

<h3><strong>Why the full-mark answer is stronger:</strong></h3>  
<ul>  
<li>It clearly shows cause → effect → consequence.</li>  
<li>Uses specific Treaty terms and links them to effects on Germany.</li>  
<li>Explains the wider significance in German politics.</li>  
</ul>

24 — KEY WORDS  
Paste into: Key words  

<strong>Treaty of Versailles</strong> – The 1919 agreement that officially ended WW1 and set terms for Germany.  
<strong>War Guilt Clause</strong> – Article 231 blaming Germany for starting WW1.  
<strong>Reparations</strong> – Payments Germany had to make to the Allies for war damage.  
<strong>Disarmament</strong> – Restrictions on Germany’s military size.  
<strong>Territorial losses</strong> – Land Germany lost as a result of the Treaty.  
<strong>League of Nations</strong> – International organisation created to keep peace.  
<strong>Diktat</strong> – A treaty imposed on a defeated country without negotiation.  
<strong>Self-determination</strong> – The principle that peoples should govern themselves.  
<strong>Armistice</strong> – An agreement to stop fighting, preceding the Treaty.  
<strong>Big Three</strong> – The main Allied leaders at Versailles: Clemenceau, Lloyd George, and Wilson.
