3 — DEFINITION  
Paste into: Text (concept)

<h2><strong>Definition</strong></h2>  
<p>👉 <strong>Electric current is the flow of electric charge, measured in amperes (A).</strong></p>  
<p>👉 <strong>Potential difference (voltage) is the energy transferred per unit charge, measured in volts (V).</strong></p>  
<p>👉 <strong>Resistance is a measure of how difficult it is for current to flow through a component, measured in ohms (Ω).</strong></p>

4 — WHY IT MATTERS  
Paste into: Text (concept)

<h2><strong>Why it matters</strong></h2>  
<p>👉 <strong>Understanding electricity lets us design and control safe, efficient devices and know why components heat up or fail in circuits.</strong></p>

5 — CORE MODEL  
Paste into: Core rule (key idea)

<p>👉 <strong>Voltage → pushes charge through a resistor → causes current → limited by resistance → affects energy transfer in a circuit.</strong></p>  
<p>This is the relationship described in Ohm’s law: <em>V = I × R</em>.</p>

9 — CORE TEACHING  
Paste into: Text (concept)

<h2><strong>Teaching: Electricity</strong></h2>  
<p>👉 Let’s break down the flow of electricity in a circuit and what controls it.</p>  

<h3><strong>Key idea:</strong></h3>  
<p>Electric current flows when there is a potential difference pushing charges through a resistor, controlled by resistance.</p>  

<h3><strong>What happens:</strong></h3>  
<ul>  
<li>Voltage (V) from the power supply pushes electrons through the circuit.</li>  
<li>Current (I) is the rate of flow of charge; if resistance is high, fewer charges flow per second.</li>  
</ul>  

<h3><strong>Structure → function / Process → effect:</strong></h3>  
<ul>  
<li>A battery creates a potential difference → causing charge to flow (current) through the circuit.</li>  
<li>The filament in a bulb resists the flow of charge → heating up and emitting light.</li>  
<li>Increasing resistance lowers current for a given voltage → reducing bulb brightness.</li>  
<li>Adding more resistors in series increases total resistance → decreasing current.</li>  
<li>In parallel circuits, voltage is the same across branches → while current splits.</li>  
</ul>  

<h3><strong>Think like an examiner:</strong></h3>  
<p>👉 Use the cause (voltage pushes charge) → effect (current flows) → outcome (device works or brightness changes) chain for marks.</p>  
<p>👉 Explain how resistance affects current using clear terms like “limits charges flowing.”</p>

12 — COMMON MISTAKE  
Paste into: Common mistake

<p>👉 A common mistake is to say “voltage is current” or confuse them. Voltage is the push that moves charge; current is the actual flow of charge.</p>  
<p>👉 Another error is using Ohm’s law without understanding that resistance limits current for a given voltage.</p>  
<p>👉 Do NOT just write formulas. Explain what each term means physically for full marks.</p>

16 — EXAM TIP  
Paste into: Exam tip (concept)

<h2><strong>🌍 Why this matters</strong></h2>  
<p>👉 Understanding how voltage, current, and resistance connect helps explain how devices work and why components can fail or overheat.</p>

<h2><strong>🎯 Premium Exam Tip</strong></h2>  
<p>👉 When explaining questions, don’t just state facts. Link cause → process → effect clearly. For example, say: “Increasing resistance reduces current because it opposes charge flow, so less energy is transferred per second.”</p>

<h2><strong>🎯 Answer Quality Modelling</strong></h2>  
<p>👉 This is how to move from a basic answer to a full-mark explanation.</p>

<h3><strong>Weak answer:</strong></h3>  
<p>Current goes down if resistance goes up.</p>

<h3><strong>Better answer:</strong></h3>  
<p>Current goes down because resistance makes it harder for charges to flow.</p>

<h3><strong>Full-mark answer:</strong></h3>  
<p>Current decreases because resistance opposes the flow of charge, reducing the number of charges passing a point each second for a given voltage.</p>

<h3><strong>Why the full-mark answer is stronger:</strong></h3>  
<ul>  
<li>It explains the cause (resistance opposes flow).</li>  
<li>Defines current properly as charge flow.</li>  
<li>Links cause → process → effect clearly and precisely.</li>  
</ul>

17 — WORKED EXAMPLE  
Paste into: Worked example (checkpoint)

Question:  
A circuit contains a 12 V battery and a resistor of 6 Ω. Calculate the current flowing through the resistor.

Answer:  
<details>  
<summary>Reveal Answer</summary>  

Use Ohm’s law: \( I = \frac{V}{R} = \frac{12\,V}{6\,\Omega} = 2\,A \).  
So, the current flowing is 2 amperes.  

</details>

22 — EXAM PRACTICE  
Paste into: Text (concept)

<p>Here’s a set of practice questions to test your understanding:</p>

<p><strong>Q1 (1 mark):</strong> What unit is electric current measured in?</p>  
<p><strong>Q2 (2 marks):</strong> Explain what happens to the current if the voltage is doubled but the resistance stays the same.</p>  
<p><strong>Q3 (3 marks):</strong> A resistor has a resistance of 4 Ω and carries a current of 3 A. Calculate the potential difference across it and explain the meaning of your answer.</p>  
<p><strong>Q4 (4 marks):</strong> Compare the current and voltage in series and parallel circuits. Explain how the total resistance changes in each.</p>

<details>  
<summary>Reveal Model Answers</summary>  

<p><strong>Q2 Answer:</strong> Doubling the voltage increases the current because current is directly proportional to voltage when resistance is constant (Ohm’s law: \(I = \frac{V}{R}\)).</p>  

<p><strong>Q3 Answer:</strong> Calculate \(V = I × R = 3\,A × 4\,Ω = 12\,V\). This means each coulomb of charge gains 12 joules of energy passing through the resistor.</p>  

<p><strong>Q4 Answer:</strong> In series, current is the same through all components but voltage splits. Total resistance increases as resistors add up. In parallel, voltage is the same across all branches but current splits. Total resistance decreases because additional paths allow more current.</p>  

</details>

24 — KEY WORDS  
Paste into: Key words

<strong>Current</strong> – The flow of electric charge; measured in amperes (A).  
<strong>Potential difference</strong> – Energy transferred per coulomb of charge; measured in volts (V).  
<strong>Resistance</strong> – A measure of how much a component opposes current; measured in ohms (Ω).  
<strong>Ohm’s law</strong> – The relationship \(V = I × R\) between voltage (V), current (I), and resistance (R).  
<strong>Voltage source</strong> – A device like a battery that provides potential difference.  
<strong>Series circuit</strong> – A circuit where components are connected end to end, so current is the same through each.  
<strong>Parallel circuit</strong> – A circuit with multiple branches where voltage is the same across each branch.  
<strong>Ammeter</strong> – A device used to measure current, connected in series.  
<strong>Voltmeter</strong> – A device used to measure potential difference, connected in parallel.  
<strong>Energy transfer</strong> – The process where electrical energy is converted to other forms like light or heat in a resistor.
