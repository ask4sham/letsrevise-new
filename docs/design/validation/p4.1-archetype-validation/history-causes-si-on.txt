9 — CORE TEACHING  
Paste into: Text (concept)

<h2><strong>Teaching: Causes of WW1</strong></h2>  

<p>👉 <strong>Key idea:</strong></p>  
<p>The First World War had multiple causes, linked together over decades before finally erupting.</p>  

<h3><strong>Long-term causes (1900s–1914):</strong></h3>  
<ul>  
<li><strong>Militarism:</strong> Countries built up armies and navies, competing to be the strongest. This created fear and readiness to solve problems with force.</li>  
<li><strong>Alliance systems:</strong> Europe split into two main groups: the Triple Alliance (Germany, Austria-Hungary, Italy) and the Triple Entente (France, Russia, Britain), meaning conflicts could drag more countries in.</li>  
<li><strong>Imperialism:</strong> Competition over colonies increased tensions, especially between Britain, France, and Germany.</li>  
<li><strong>Nationalism:</strong> Strong pride and desire for independence caused unrest within and between nations, especially in the Balkans.</li>  
</ul>  

<h3><strong>Short-term causes (1912–1914):</strong></h3>  
<ul>  
<li>Serbia wanted to unite Slavic people but Austria-Hungary opposed this, causing crises like the Bosnian Crisis and Balkan Wars.</li>  
<li>Diplomatic tensions rose as countries took sides.</li>  
</ul>  

<h3><strong>Trigger event (June 1914):</strong></h3>  
<ul>  
<li>The assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand by a Serbian nationalist sparked Austria-Hungary’s harsh response.</li>  
<li>Due to alliances, a local conflict quickly escalated into a full-scale war.</li>  
</ul>  

<h3><strong>Think like an examiner:</strong></h3>  
<p>👉 When you explain causes, separate long-term and short-term causes, and identify the trigger event clearly, linking cause → effect → outcome.</p>

12 — COMMON MISTAKE  
Paste into: Common mistake

<p>👉 A common mistake is saying <em>"WW1 was caused just by the assassination."</em></p>  
<p>This is wrong because it ignores the build-up of tensions over many years that made Europe ready for war.</p>  
<p>👉 A better way to say it is: The assassination was the <strong>trigger</strong>, but long-term causes like alliances and militarism created the conditions for war.</p>  
<p>This approach earns marks for understanding complexity, not simple cause → effect answers.</p>

16 — EXAM TIP  
Paste into: Exam tip (concept)

<h2><strong>🌍 Why this matters</strong></h2>  
<p>👉 <strong>Understanding the causes of WW1 is essential because exam questions often ask you to explain, describe or evaluate causes – and marks are available for clear cause → effect chains.</strong></p>  
<p>👉 You must separate <em>long-term causes</em> (slow build-up over years), <em>short-term causes</em> (crises that increased tension), and the <em>trigger</em> (the final spark).</p>  

<h2><strong>🎯 Premium Exam Tip</strong></h2>  
<p>👉 When answering explain or evaluate questions, use this structure:</p>  
<ul>  
<li>State the cause clearly.</li>  
<li>Explain how or why that cause increased tension or led to war.</li>  
<li>Link the cause to the eventual outbreak of WW1 with a clear cause → effect.</li>  
<li>For evaluation, compare causes and say which was most important.</li>  
</ul>  

<h2><strong>🎯 Answer Quality Modelling</strong></h2>  
<p>👉 This is how to move from a basic answer to a full-mark explanation.</p>  

<h3><strong>Weak answer:</strong></h3>  
<p>WW1 started because Franz Ferdinand was killed.</p>  

<h3><strong>Better answer:</strong></h3>  
<p>WW1 started because the assassination of Franz Ferdinand caused Austria-Hungary to declare war on Serbia, which caused other countries to join.</p>  

<h3><strong>Full-mark answer:</strong></h3>  
<p>WW1 was triggered by the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand, which led Austria-Hungary to declare war on Serbia. However, long-term causes like militarism and alliances meant that once Austria-Hungary acted, many countries were pulled in due to their commitments, causing a general European war.</p>  

<h3><strong>Why the full-mark answer is stronger:</strong></h3>  
<ul>  
<li>It explains the trigger and links it to the wider long-term causes.</li>  
<li>It uses specific terminology like "trigger" and "long-term causes."</li>  
<li>It shows the causal chain rather than just stating an event.</li>  
</ul>  

17 — WORKED EXAMPLE  
Paste into: Worked example (checkpoint)

Question:  
Explain why the alliance system was a cause of WW1. (4 marks)

Answer:  
<details>  
<summary>Reveal Answer</summary>

1. The alliance system was a long-term cause because it divided Europe into two rival groups (Triple Alliance and Triple Entente).  
2. This caused tension as countries promised to protect each other, increasing fear and suspicion.  
3. When Austria-Hungary declared war on Serbia after the assassination, allies were pulled in automatically, causing the war to spread quickly.  
4. Therefore, alliances made a local conflict into a full-scale European war, directly contributing to the outbreak of WW1.  
</details>

24 — KEY WORDS  
Paste into: Key words

<strong>Long-term cause</strong> – A factor that builds up over many years increasing tension.  
<strong>Short-term cause</strong> – A factor that increases tension just before the outbreak of war.  
<strong>Trigger</strong> – The event that sets off a conflict immediately.  
<strong>Militarism</strong> – The building up of armed forces to prepare for war.  
<strong>Alliance</strong> – An agreement between countries to support each other.  
<strong>Nationalism</strong> – Pride in one’s nation, sometimes leading to desire for independence or conflict.  
<strong>Imperialism</strong> – The competition for colonies and global power.  
<strong>Diplomacy</strong> – Negotiations between countries to resolve issues.  
<strong>Evaluate</strong> – To judge the importance or effectiveness of something using evidence.  
<strong>Explain</strong> – To make something clear by describing causes and effects in detail.
